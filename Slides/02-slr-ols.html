Warm-up

Recall Week 1. Why can’t we simply compare the wages of people with 12 and 16 years of education to learn the return to education?

A) Because wages are measured in dollars

B) Because we don’t have enough observations

C) Because other factors (e.g., ability) that affect wages may differ between the two groups

D) Because education is a discrete variable

The ceteris paribus condition fails if unobserved factors differ systematically across education levels. Today we give this idea a precise mathematical form: the zero conditional mean assumption E(u\mid x)=0.

The model

The population model, the key assumption E(u\mid x) = 0, and the population regression function.

The simple regression model

\Large \color{#14324f}{y} = \color{#1f5fa8}{\beta_0} + \color{#1f5fa8}{\beta_1}\,\color{#14324f}{x} + \color{#c0392b}{u}

y x \beta_0 \beta_1 u

Click each symbol to see its name and role.

  • Also called the bivariate or two-variable linear regression model.
  • Goal: explain y by x, and in particular measure how y changes when x changes, holding u fixed: \Delta y = \beta_1 \Delta x \quad \text{if}\quad \Delta u = 0

Two examples

Agriculture

yield = \beta_0 + \beta_1\, fertilizer + u

  • \beta_1: the change in wheat yield when fertilizer increases by one unit, ceteris paribus
  • u: land quality, rainfall, parasites, …

Labor economics

wage = \beta_0 + \beta_1\, educ + u

  • \beta_1: the change in the hourly wage from one more year of education, ceteris paribus
  • u: experience, ability, tenure, gender, quality of education, …

Linearity has a price

A one-unit change in x has the same effect on y regardless of the starting value of x. Is the 16th year of education worth the same as the 8th? Probably not. Next week we allow for nonlinearities (logs, quadratics).

When can we trust a ceteris paribus conclusion?

We need assumptions on how the unobservables u relate to x.

Assumption 1: E(u) = 0

  • On average, the unobservables are zero.
  • It is not restrictive: as long as the model has an intercept, we can always redefine \beta_0 so that it holds.

Assumption 2: E(u\mid x) = E(u) = 0

  • Zero conditional mean (mean independence)
  • The average of u is the same in every slice of the population defined by x.
  • This is the crucial assumption.

Zero correlation is not enough

\text{Corr}(x,u)=0 only rules out a linear relationship. E(u\mid x)=0 also implies that u is uncorrelated with every function of x, such as x^2 or \sqrt{x}.

Zero conditional mean: two examples

Wage equation

Suppose u is mainly innate ability. Then SLR.4 requires

\begin{aligned} E(abil\mid educ=8) &= E(abil\mid educ=12) \\ &= \dots = 0 \end{aligned}

If more able people choose more education, the assumption fails.

Fertilizer experiment

  • If fertilizer amounts are assigned independently of land quality: the assumption holds ✓
  • If better plots get more fertilizer: E(u\mid x) increases with x ✗

Check: when does the assumption hold?

In which case is E(u\mid x) = 0 most plausible?

A) x = police officers per capita, y = city crime rate

B) x = a scholarship amount assigned by lottery, y = exam score

C) x = years of education, y = wage, u contains ability

A lottery makes x independent of everything else, so it works like an experiment. In (A) cities with more crime hire more police (simultaneity). In (C) ability is correlated with education.

The population regression function (PRF)

Take the conditional expectation of the model and use E(u\mid x)=0:

E(y\mid x) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x + \underbrace{E(u\mid x)}_{=\,0} = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x

Systematic and unsystematic parts

Under E(u\mid x)=0, the dependent variable splits into two parts:

y = \underbrace{\beta_0 + \beta_1 x}_{\text{systematic part } E(y|x)} + \underbrace{u}_{\text{unsystematic part}}

  • Systematic part: the part of y explained by x
  • Unsystematic part: the part of y that cannot be explained by x

Where we are going

The PRF is unknown, because \beta_0 and \beta_1 are unknown. With a random sample, we will estimate it.

Estimation

Two routes to the same formulas — method of moments and least squares — and what the residuals are.

The estimation problem

We have a random sample of size n from the population: \{(x_i, y_i): i = 1, \dots, n\}

The model holds for every observation:

y_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_i + u_i, \qquad i = 1, 2, \dots, n

\begin{aligned} y_1 &= \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_1 + u_1\\ y_2 &= \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_2 + u_2\\ &\;\;\vdots\\ y_n &= \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_n + u_n \end{aligned}

  • n equations, 2 unknown parameters, and n unknown errors
  • We cannot “solve” this system exactly.
  • We need a principle to choose \hat\beta_0 and \hat\beta_1.

Two routes, one answer:

  1. method of moments
  2. least squares

Route 1: method of moments

Our assumptions imply two population moment conditions. Since E(u\mid x)=0, we have E(u)=0 and \text{Cov}(x,u)=E(xu)=0:

E(y - \beta_0 - \beta_1 x) = 0, \qquad E\big[x\,(y - \beta_0 - \beta_1 x)\big] = 0

Replace the population expectations with sample averages:

\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n \big(y_i - \hat\beta_0 - \hat\beta_1 x_i\big) = 0, \qquad \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n x_i\big(y_i - \hat\beta_0 - \hat\beta_1 x_i\big) = 0

That gives two equations in two unknowns. From the first one:

\bar y = \hat\beta_0 + \hat\beta_1 \bar x \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \boxed{\hat\beta_0 = \bar y - \hat\beta_1 \bar x}

Notation: hats mean estimators. \hat\beta_0, \hat\beta_1 are functions of the data and change from sample to sample.

The slope estimator

Substituting \hat\beta_0 into the second condition and using \sum x_i(x_i-\bar x) = \sum (x_i-\bar x)^2 and \sum x_i(y_i-\bar y) = \sum (x_i-\bar x)(y_i-\bar y):

\boxed{\;\hat\beta_1 = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i-\bar x)(y_i-\bar y)}{\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i-\bar x)^2} = \frac{\widehat{\text{Cov}}(x,y)}{\widehat{\text{Var}}(x)}\;}

  1. The slope is the sample covariance of x and y divided by the sample variance of x.
  2. Its sign equals the sign of the sample correlation between x and y.
  1. We need variation in x: \textstyle\sum_{i}(x_i-\bar x)^2 > 0 If everyone in the sample has 12 years of education, we cannot learn the return to education.

Check your understanding

A tiny sample: x = (1, 2, 3) and y = (2, 3, 3). Compute \hat\beta_1.

\bar x = 2, \bar y = 8/3. The deviations of x are (-1,0,1) and the deviations of y are (-2/3, 1/3, 1/3). \sum (x_i-\bar x)(y_i-\bar y) = 2/3 + 0 + 1/3 = 1 and \sum (x_i-\bar x)^2 = 2, so \hat\beta_1 = 0.5. Then \hat\beta_0 = 8/3 - 0.5\times 2 = 1.667.

Check: no variation in x

In a sample of 200 workers, all have exactly 12 years of education. What happens when we regress wage on educ?

A) \hat\beta_1 = 0

B) \hat\beta_1 is very large

C) \hat\beta_1 equals the average wage

D) \hat\beta_1 cannot be computed

The denominator \sum (x_i-\bar x)^2 = 0, so the slope is undefined. R would report NA for the coefficient.

Route 2: ordinary least squares

For any candidate line, define the fitted values and residuals:

\hat y_i = \hat\beta_0 + \hat\beta_1 x_i, \qquad \hat u_i = y_i - \hat y_i = y_i - \hat\beta_0 - \hat\beta_1 x_i

OLS chooses the line that makes the sum of squared residuals as small as possible:

\min_{\hat\beta_0,\,\hat\beta_1}\; SSR = \sum_{i=1}^n \big(y_i - \hat\beta_0 - \hat\beta_1 x_i\big)^2

First-order conditions:

\frac{\partial SSR}{\partial \hat\beta_0} = -2\sum_{i=1}^n \big(y_i - \hat\beta_0 - \hat\beta_1 x_i\big) = 0, \qquad \frac{\partial SSR}{\partial \hat\beta_1} = -2\sum_{i=1}^n x_i\big(y_i - \hat\beta_0 - \hat\beta_1 x_i\big) = 0

These are the sample moment conditions multiplied by -2n, so the two routes give the same estimator.

Interactive: be the OLS estimator

Fit the line by hand by making the red residuals, or their squares, as small as you can. Then reveal OLS.

Class game: ask two students to compete, one on the small data set and one on gpa1. Then switch on the squared residuals. Large residuals dominate SSR, which explains why OLS is sensitive to outliers (Week 13).

Why squared residuals?

  • Positive and negative residuals would cancel out if we just added them up.
  • Squaring gives large errors more weight.
  • It gives closed-form solutions, which are easy to compute and to study.
  • Under the assumptions of the coming weeks, OLS has very attractive statistical properties (unbiasedness, efficiency).

Minimizing \sum \hat u_i (not squared) would…

A) give the OLS line

B) not have a unique solution, because any line through (\bar x,\bar y) gives \sum \hat u_i = 0

C) give a line with a steeper slope

Every line through the point of means has residuals that sum to zero, so this criterion cannot pick a slope.

Error vs. residual — don’t mix them up!

Error u_i

  • Belongs to the population model
  • u_i = y_i - \beta_0 - \beta_1 x_i
  • Never observed, because the \beta’s are unknown

Residual \hat u_i

  • Belongs to the estimated model
  • \hat u_i = y_i - \hat\beta_0 - \hat\beta_1 x_i
  • Computed from the data after estimation
Population (unknown) Sample (estimated)
PRF: E(y\mid x) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x SRF: \hat y = \hat\beta_0 + \hat\beta_1 x
parameters \beta_0, \beta_1 (fixed numbers) estimates \hat\beta_0, \hat\beta_1 (vary with the sample)
error u residual \hat u

The PRF is unique and unknown. The SRF is its estimate and changes with every sample.

Interactive: one PRF, many SRFs

Preview of Week 3: the SRFs scatter around the PRF, and the average of the slope estimates is close to 0.5. That is unbiasedness. With a larger n they scatter less (variance).

Example: CEO salaries

One regression from start to finish: estimates, interpretation, fitted values and residuals.

CEO salary and return on equity

salary = \beta_0 + \beta_1\, roe + u

salary: annual CEO salary in $1000s; roe: average return on equity over the last three years (%); n = 209 firms.

data("ceosal1")

\widehat{salary} = 963.191 + 18.501\, roe

Interpretation: if the return on equity rises by one percentage point (\Delta roe = 1), salary is predicted to rise by 18.501, that is $18,501, ceteris paribus.

Algebraic properties of OLS

Properties that hold in every sample, the SST = SSE + SSR decomposition, and R^2.

Three properties that always hold

These follow directly from the first-order conditions. They hold in every sample and do not depend on any assumption.

  1. Residuals sum (and average) to zero: \textstyle\sum_{i=1}^n \hat u_i = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; \bar{\hat u} = 0

  2. Residuals are uncorrelated with x: \textstyle\sum_{i=1}^n x_i \hat u_i = 0

  3. The point (\bar x, \bar y) is always on the OLS line, and \bar y = \overline{\hat y}.

Where do they come from?

Properties 1 and 2 are exactly the two first-order conditions. Property 3 follows from \hat\beta_0 = \bar y - \hat\beta_1 \bar x.

Interactive: try to break the properties

Drag the points anywhere you like. Can you make \sum \hat u_i or \sum x_i \hat u_i different from zero?

Sums of squares

Every observation splits into a fitted part and a residual part: \;y_i = \hat y_i + \hat u_i

\begin{aligned} SST &= \textstyle\sum_{i}(y_i - \bar y)^2 && \text{total}\\ SSE &= \textstyle\sum_{i}(\hat y_i - \bar y)^2 && \text{explained}\\ SSR &= \textstyle\sum_{i}\hat u_i^2 && \text{residual} \end{aligned}

\boxed{\,SST = SSE + SSR\,}

Note that \widehat{\text{Var}}(y) = SST/(n-1).

Goodness of fit: the coefficient of determination

R^2 = \frac{SSE}{SST} = 1 - \frac{SSR}{SST} = \big[\text{Corr}(y,\hat y)\big]^2, \qquad 0 \le R^2 \le 1

R^2 is the fraction of the sample variation in y that is explained by x. Multiplied by 100, it is a percentage.

Interactive: decomposing the variation

Example: college GPA and high-school GPA

data("gpa1")
gpareg <- lm(colGPA ~ hsGPA, data = gpa1)
summary(gpareg)$r.squared
#> [1] 0.1718563
var(fitted(gpareg)) / var(gpa1$colGPA)      # SSE/SST
#> [1] 0.1718563
cor(fitted(gpareg), gpa1$colGPA)^2          # squared correlation
#> [1] 0.1718563

\widehat{colGPA} = 1.42 + 0.48\, hsGPA, \qquad R^2 = 0.172

About 17.2% of the variation in college GPA is explained by variation in high-school GPA.

R² — common misconceptions

The CEO regression has R^2 = 0.013. What can we conclude?

A) The regression is wrong and should be discarded

B) roe has no causal effect on salary

C) roe explains only about 1.3% of the variation in salaries. Many other factors matter.

D) The OLS estimates are biased

A low R^2 is common with cross-sectional data and does not make the estimates biased or useless. R^2 measures fit, not causality. Whether \hat\beta_1 is a good estimate of the causal effect depends on E(u\mid x)=0, not on R^2.

Check: an identity for R^2

Which is always true for OLS with an intercept?

A) R^2 increases with the sample size

B) R^2 = [\text{Corr}(y,\hat y)]^2

C) A high R^2 means x causes y

This identity always holds for OLS with an intercept. Neither (A) nor (C) holds in general.

Key takeaways

  1. y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x + u: the error u holds everything else.
  2. E(u\mid x)=0 is the key to ceteris paribus interpretation. It gives the PRF E(y\mid x)=\beta_0+\beta_1 x.
  3. OLS minimizes the SSR: \hat\beta_1 = \widehat{\text{Cov}}(x,y)/\widehat{\text{Var}}(x) and \hat\beta_0=\bar y-\hat\beta_1\bar x.
  4. The residuals sum to zero, are uncorrelated with x, and the line passes through (\bar x,\bar y).
  5. SST = SSE + SSR. R^2 measures fit, not causality.
