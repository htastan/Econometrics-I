Previously

The LPM is always heteroskedastic, which is why we promised robust standard errors.

Warm-up: what exactly goes wrong?

MLR.5 (homoskedasticity) says \text{Var}(u \mid x_1,\dots,x_k) = \sigma^2. Suppose it fails.

Which statement is correct?

A) OLS becomes biased, so the estimates are worthless

B) OLS stays unbiased and consistent, but the usual standard errors, t and F statistics are invalid

C) Nothing happens: heteroskedasticity only matters in time series

Unbiasedness and consistency come from MLR.1–MLR.4, which do not involve the variance. What breaks is inference: the formula \text{Var}(\hat\beta_j) = \sigma^2/[SST_j(1-R_j^2)] is no longer valid, so every t and F test based on it is wrong. OLS also stops being BLUE.

Consequences

What still works, what breaks, and the repair: robust standard errors.

Where heteroskedasticity comes from

  • Savings and income: low-income households can save little; high-income households vary a lot.
  • Wages and education: the dispersion of wages grows with education.
  • Firm size: the variance of profits grows with size.
  • The LPM: \text{Var}(u\mid \mathbf x) = p(\mathbf x)[1-p(\mathbf x)] by construction.

It is the rule rather than the exception in cross-sectional data.

In the simple regression model, if \text{Var}(u_i\mid x_i) = \sigma_i^2,

\text{Var}(\hat\beta_1) = \frac{\sum_i (x_i-\bar x)^2\sigma_i^2}{\left[\sum_i (x_i-\bar x)^2\right]^2}

which equals the familiar \sigma^2/SST_x only if all \sigma_i^2 are equal.

Interactive: unbiased, but wrongly measured

Set gamma = 0: homoskedastic, the three bars are equal and both tests have size 5%. Increase gamma: the true sd of beta1-hat rises, the usual se does not follow (here it underestimates), and the usual t test rejects the true null far too often, while the robust test stays near 5%. The mean of beta1-hat stays at 0.5 throughout.

Where the robust formula comes from

Start from \;\hat\beta_1 = \beta_1 + \dfrac{\sum_i (x_i-\bar x)u_i}{SST_x}\; and take the variance:

\text{Var}(\hat\beta_1) = \frac{\sum_i (x_i-\bar x)^2\,\sigma_i^2}{SST_x^2} \qquad\xrightarrow{\ \text{if } \sigma_i^2=\sigma^2\ }\qquad \frac{\sigma^2}{SST_x}

The usual standard error estimates the right-hand expression, which is why it is wrong when the \sigma_i^2 differ.

We cannot estimate n separate variances \sigma_i^2. White’s (1980) insight: we do not have to, because only the sum matters. Replacing each \sigma_i^2 by the squared residual \hat u_i^2 gives a consistent estimator of that sum:

\widehat{\text{Var}}(\hat\beta_1) = \frac{\sum_i (x_i-\bar x)^2\,\hat u_i^2}{SST_x^2}

Each \hat u_i^2 is a hopeless estimate of its own \sigma_i^2, but the average behaves well as n grows.

Heteroskedasticity-robust standard errors

With k regressors, (x_i - \bar x) is replaced by the part of x_j left after partialling out the other regressors:

\widehat{\text{Var}}(\hat\beta_j) = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^n \hat r_{ij}^2\,\hat u_i^2}{SSR_j^2}

where \hat r_{ij} are the residuals from regressing x_j on all other regressors, and SSR_j = \sum_i \hat r_{ij}^2.

  • Also called White, Huber–White or Eicker–Huber–White standard errors.
  • They are valid in large samples whether or not MLR.5 holds. With them we also get robust t, F and LM statistics.
  • Nothing changes in the coefficients: only the standard errors change.

Robust standard errors in R

h1 <- lm(price ~ lotsize + sqrft + bdrms, data = hprice1)
round(cbind(usual = coef(summary(h1))[, 2],
            robust = sqrt(diag(vcovHC(h1, type = "HC1")))), 4)
#>               usual  robust
#> (Intercept) 29.4750 37.1382
#> lotsize      0.0006  0.0013
#> sqrft        0.0132  0.0177
#> bdrms        9.0101  8.4786
coeftest(h1, vcov = vcovHC(h1, type = "HC1"))[2, ]     # lotsize with robust se
#>    Estimate  Std. Error     t value    Pr(>|t|) 
#> 0.002067707 0.001251424 1.652282516 0.102210357

The coefficient on lotsize is unchanged, but its t falls from 3.22 to 1.65: with valid standard errors it is no longer significant at 5%.

The same result in equation form

Usual standard errors in parentheses, robust standard errors in brackets:

\begin{aligned} \widehat{price} &= - \underset{\small [37.14]}{\underset{\small (29.48)}{21.77}} + \underset{\small [0.00125]}{\underset{\small (0.000642)}{0.00207}}\,lotsize + \underset{\small [0.0177]}{\underset{\small (0.0132)}{0.123}}\,sqrft \\ & + \underset{\small [8.48]}{\underset{\small (9.01)}{13.85}}\,bdrms \\ & n = 88,\quad R^2 = 0.672 \end{aligned}

  • The estimates are identical: only the numbers underneath change.
  • lotsize: t = 0.00207/0.00064 = 3.22 with the usual se, but 0.00207/0.00125 = 1.65 with the robust se.

Modern practice

Many applied papers report robust standard errors by default for cross-sectional data. They cost nothing when MLR.5 happens to hold (in large samples).

Testing for heteroskedasticity

Two tests, one logic

Both tests ask the same question — is u^2 related to the regressors? — and both follow the same three steps. They differ only in what goes on the right-hand side of the auxiliary regression.

Breusch–Pagan

\hat u^2 on x_1,\dots,x_k

Detects variance that moves linearly with the regressors. Cheap: q = k restrictions.

White

\hat u^2 on the x_j, their squares and cross products (or on \hat y,\hat y^2)

Detects more general patterns, at the cost of many more restrictions.

In both cases: reject H_0 = find evidence of heteroskedasticity.

The Breusch–Pagan test

H_0: \text{Var}(u\mid x_1,\dots,x_k) = \sigma^2 \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad H_0: E(u^2 \mid x_1, \dots, x_k) = \sigma^2

H_1:\; E(u^2\mid x_1,\dots,x_k) \text{ depends on at least one } x_j

We cannot see u, so we use \hat u:

  1. Estimate the model by OLS and save the residuals \hat u_i.
  2. Regress \hat u_i^2 on x_1, \dots, x_k and keep its R^2_{\hat u^2}.
  3. Test the joint significance of all k regressors in that auxiliary regression: F = \frac{R^2_{\hat u^2}/k}{(1-R^2_{\hat u^2})/(n-k-1)} \sim F_{k,\,n-k-1} \qquad\text{or}\qquad LM = n\,R^2_{\hat u^2} \stackrel{a}{\sim} \chi^2_k

Reject H_0 (that is, find heteroskedasticity) if the statistic is large.

The White test

The condition needed for the usual OLS statistics to be valid asymptotically is weaker: u^2 must be uncorrelated with the x_j, their squares and their cross products. The White test puts all of these in the auxiliary regression.

H_0: E(u^2\mid \mathbf x) = \sigma^2 \qquad\text{vs.}\qquad H_1: E(u^2\mid \mathbf x) \text{ depends on the } x_j,\ x_j^2 \text{ or } x_jx_h

  1. OLS, save \hat u_i.   2. Regress \hat u_i^2 on all of those terms.   3. LM = n R^2_{\hat u^2} \stackrel{a}{\sim} \chi^2_q, or the F form.

For k = 3 the auxiliary regression is

\begin{aligned} \hat u^2 = \delta_0 &+ \textstyle\sum_{j}\delta_j x_j\\ &+ \textstyle\sum_j \gamma_j x_j^2\\ &+ \textstyle\sum_{j<h}\theta_{jh}\, x_jx_h + error \end{aligned}

That is q = 9 restrictions already, and q = k(k+3)/2 in general: the test uses up many degrees of freedom.

Special (simplified) form: use the fitted values, which summarize all the x’s:

\hat u^2 = \delta_0 + \delta_1 \hat y + \delta_2 \hat y^2 + error

H_0: \delta_1 = \delta_2 = 0, \qquad q = 2

Only 2 restrictions, whatever k is. Useful when the variance is thought to depend on E(y\mid \mathbf x).

Interactive: the tests on real data

Level-level with Breusch-Pagan: LM = 14.09, p = 0.003, strong evidence of heteroskedasticity. Switch to the log-log model: LM = 4.22, p = 0.24, no evidence. Then the White special form: 3.45, p = 0.18. This is the main practical message: taking logs often cures the problem.

The same tests in R

Logs and the White test

#> 
#>  studentized Breusch-Pagan test
#> 
#> data:  h2
#> BP = 3.4473, df = 2, p-value = 0.1784

A caveat

These tests assume the model is otherwise correctly specified. A wrong functional form can show up as “heteroskedasticity”, so check the functional form first (Week 13).

Weighted least squares

If we are willing to model the variance, we can do better than OLS: WLS and FGLS.

If we knew the form of the variance

Suppose \;\text{Var}(u_i \mid \mathbf x_i) = \sigma^2 h(\mathbf x_i)\; with h(\cdot) > 0 known. Divide everything by \sqrt{h_i}:

\frac{y_i}{\sqrt{h_i}} = \beta_0\frac{1}{\sqrt{h_i}} + \beta_1\frac{x_{i1}}{\sqrt{h_i}} + \dots + \frac{u_i}{\sqrt{h_i}}

The transformed error has constant variance:

\text{Var}\!\left(\frac{u_i}{\sqrt{h_i}}\,\Big|\, \mathbf x_i\right) = \frac{\sigma^2 h_i}{h_i} = \sigma^2

OLS on the transformed model is weighted least squares (WLS): it minimizes \sum_i \hat u_i^2 / h_i, giving less weight to noisy observations. Under MLR.1–4 plus this variance form, WLS is BLUE. Coefficients keep their original interpretation.

Interactive: OLS vs WLS

Feasible GLS: when h is unknown

In practice we must estimate h(\mathbf x). A flexible and always-positive choice:

\text{Var}(u\mid \mathbf x) = \sigma^2 \exp(\delta_0 + \delta_1 x_1 + \dots + \delta_k x_k)

Steps (FGLS)

  1. OLS, save the residuals \hat u.
  2. Regress \log(\hat u^2) on x_1,\dots,x_k; save the fitted values \hat g.
  3. \hat h = \exp(\hat g).
  4. Run WLS with weights 1/\hat h.
  • FGLS is biased but consistent and asymptotically more efficient than OLS.
  • If the variance model is wrong, FGLS is still consistent but the efficiency gain is lost: use robust standard errors with WLS.
  • For the LPM use \hat h_i = \hat y_i(1-\hat y_i), trimming values outside [0.01, 0.99].

Example: cigarette demand (smoke)

ols <- lm(cigs ~ log(income) + log(cigpric) + educ + age + I(age^2) + restaurn,
          data = smoke)
bptest(ols, ~ fitted(ols) + I(fitted(ols)^2))     # White special form
#> 
#>  studentized Breusch-Pagan test
#> 
#> data:  ols
#> BP = 26.573, df = 2, p-value = 1.698e-06

Strong evidence of heteroskedasticity. Now estimate the variance function and run WLS with weights 1/\hat h_i:

g    <- lm(log(resid(ols)^2) ~ log(income) + log(cigpric) + educ + age +
             I(age^2) + restaurn, data = smoke)
hhat <- exp(fitted(g))
fgls <- lm(cigs ~ log(income) + log(cigpric) + educ + age + I(age^2) + restaurn,
           data = smoke, weights = 1 / hhat)

Cigarette demand: OLS vs. FGLS

OLS

\begin{aligned} \widehat{cigs} &= - \underset{\small (24.08)}{3.64} + \underset{\small (0.728)}{0.880}\,\log(income) - \underset{\small (5.77)}{0.751}\,\log(cigpric) - \underset{\small (0.167)}{0.501}\,educ \\ & + \underset{\small (0.160)}{0.771}\,age - \underset{\small (0.00174)}{0.00902}\,age^2 - \underset{\small (1.11)}{2.83}\,restaurn \end{aligned}

FGLS (weighted least squares with weights 1/\hat h_i)

\begin{aligned} \widehat{cigs} &= \underset{\small (17.80)}{5.64} + \underset{\small (0.437)}{1.30}\,\log(income) - \underset{\small (4.46)}{2.94}\,\log(cigpric) - \underset{\small (0.120)}{0.463}\,educ \\ & + \underset{\small (0.0968)}{0.482}\,age - \underset{\small (0.000939)}{0.00563}\,age^2 - \underset{\small (0.796)}{3.46}\,restaurn \end{aligned}

Reading the two sets of estimates

  • income: 0.88 (OLS, t = 1.21) vs. 1.30 (FGLS, t = 2.96): now significant.
  • educ: one more year of schooling lowers daily cigarettes by about 0.46–0.50.
  • restaurn (smoking restrictions in restaurants): -2.83 vs. -3.46 cigarettes per day.
  • The signs and magnitudes are similar; FGLS is more precise.

Which to report?

There is no universal answer. A common approach: report OLS with robust standard errors as the baseline, and WLS/FGLS when the variance model is credible and efficiency matters.

R toolkit for this topic

library(lmtest); library(sandwich)
m <- lm(price ~ lotsize + sqrft + bdrms, data = hprice1)

coeftest(m, vcov = vcovHC(m, type = "HC1"))        # robust t tests
waldtest(m, vcov = vcovHC(m, type = "HC1"))        # robust F test

bptest(m)                                          # Breusch-Pagan
bptest(m, ~ fitted(m) + I(fitted(m)^2))            # White, special form

# WLS / FGLS
g <- lm(log(resid(m)^2) ~ lotsize + sqrft + bdrms, data = hprice1)
lm(price ~ lotsize + sqrft + bdrms, data = hprice1, weights = 1 / exp(fitted(g)))

Key takeaways

  1. Heteroskedasticity does not cause bias or inconsistency; it invalidates the usual standard errors and makes OLS no longer BLUE.
  2. Robust standard errors fix inference without knowing the form of the variance. Use them by default in cross sections.
  3. Breusch–Pagan: regress \hat u^2 on the regressors. White: add squares and cross products, or use \hat y, \hat y^2.
  4. A log transformation often removes much of the problem.
  5. WLS is BLUE if the variance form is known; FGLS estimates it, at the cost of an approximation.
