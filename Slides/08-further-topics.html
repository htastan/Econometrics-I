Warm-up: is more experience always better?

From wage1: \;\widehat{wage} = 3.73 + 0.298\,exper - 0.0061\,exper^2

What does this equation say about the effect of one more year of experience?

A) Each year of experience raises the wage by $0.298

B) Each year of experience lowers the wage by $0.0061

C) The effect depends on how much experience the worker already has, and it gets smaller

With a quadratic term the slope is not constant: \Delta wage/\Delta exper \approx 0.298 - 2(0.0061)\,exper. It is about $0.30 for a beginner, $0.18 at 10 years, and it turns negative after about 24 years. Reading \hat\beta_1 alone is a classic mistake.

Units of measurement

What changes and what does not when the data are rescaled, and standardized (beta) coefficients.

What changes when we rescale the data?

Changes

  • the coefficients and their standard errors
  • SSR and \hat\sigma if y is rescaled
  • the intercept

Does not change

  • t statistics and p-values
  • R^2 and the F statistic
  • all substantive conclusions

Rescaling is cosmetic: it is used to avoid long strings of zeros and to make coefficients easier to read.

Interactive: changing the units

Birth weight on cigarettes and family income. Switch the units and watch the table:

ounces to grams multiplies every coefficient by 28.35; cigarettes to packs multiplies the smoking coefficient by 20; income in dollars divides its coefficient by 1000. The t statistics (-5.06 and 3.18) and R-squared never move.

Standardized coefficients

What if we want to compare effects measured in completely different units (pollution, crime, rooms)?

Standardize every variable: subtract the mean and divide by the standard deviation,

z_y = \frac{y - \bar y}{\hat\sigma_y}, \qquad z_j = \frac{x_j - \bar x_j}{\hat\sigma_j} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \hat z_y = \hat b_1 z_1 + \dots + \hat b_k z_k, \quad \hat b_j = \left(\frac{\hat\sigma_j}{\hat\sigma_y}\right)\hat\beta_j

Interpretation: if x_j increases by one standard deviation, y changes by \hat b_j standard deviations, ceteris paribus.

Also called beta coefficients

The original units become irrelevant, so the \hat b_j can be compared with each other.

Example: house prices (hprice2)

data("hprice2")
lvl <- lm(price ~ nox + crime + rooms + dist + stratio, data = hprice2)
std <- lm(scale(price) ~ scale(nox) + scale(crime) + scale(rooms) +
                         scale(dist) + scale(stratio), data = hprice2)
round(rbind(level = coef(lvl)[-1], standardized = coef(std)[-1]), 4)
#>                     nox     crime     rooms       dist    stratio
#> level        -2706.4326 -153.6010 6735.4983 -1026.8063 -1149.2038
#> standardized    -0.3404   -0.1433    0.5139    -0.2348    -0.2703
  • One standard deviation more pollution lowers the price by 0.34 standard deviations; one standard deviation more crime lowers it by 0.14.
  • The effect of pollution is more than twice as large as that of crime, and rooms (0.51) has the largest standardized effect.

Functional form

Logs refined, quadratics and interactions: models where the effect of x depends on x.

Logarithms: a quick reminder and one refinement

For a log-level model, 100\,\hat\beta_j is the approximate percentage change. For large changes use the exact formula:

\%\Delta \hat y = 100\,\big[\exp(\hat\beta_j) - 1\big]

Example (hprice2): \widehat{\log(price)} = 9.23 - 0.718\log(nox) + 0.306\,rooms

One more room: the approximation gives 30.6%. What does the exact formula give? (in %, one decimal)

100[\exp(0.306) - 1] = 35.8\%. The approximation understates the effect when the coefficient is large. It is a biased but consistent estimator of the percentage change.

Rules of thumb: log for positive amounts (wages, prices, sales); levels for years, rates and shares; if y \geq 0 with some zeros, \log(1+y) is an option.

Quadratic models

\hat y = \hat\beta_0 + \hat\beta_1 x + \hat\beta_2 x^2 \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \frac{\Delta \hat y}{\Delta x} \approx \hat\beta_1 + 2\hat\beta_2 x

  • The marginal effect depends on x: reading \hat\beta_1 alone is meaningless unless x = 0 is relevant.
  • \hat\beta_1 > 0,\ \hat\beta_2 < 0: concave (∩). \hat\beta_1 < 0,\ \hat\beta_2 > 0: convex (∪).
  • Turning point: \;x^* = \left|\hat\beta_1 / (2\hat\beta_2)\right|
data("wage1")
wq <- lm(wage ~ exper + I(exper^2), data = wage1)

\begin{aligned} \widehat{wage} &= \underset{\small (0.346)}{3.73} + \underset{\small (0.0410)}{0.298}\,exper - \underset{\small (0.000903)}{0.00613}\,exper^2 \\ & n = 526,\quad R^2 = 0.0928 \end{aligned}

Interactive: quadratic models

wage: the tangent is steep at 0, flatter at 10, flat at the turning point of 24.3 years and negative afterwards. Ask whether we believe wages fall after 24 years of experience: only 28% of the sample lies beyond it, and other variables are omitted here. house prices: the relationship is U-shaped, with a turning point at 4.4 rooms; below it there are almost no observations, so in the relevant range the effect of rooms is positive and increasing.

Turning points: always ask whether they matter

hprice2, with \log(price) on \log(nox), \log(dist), rooms, rooms^2 and stratio:

hq <- lm(log(price) ~ log(nox) + log(dist) + rooms + I(rooms^2) + stratio,
         data = hprice2)
round(coef(hq)[c("rooms", "I(rooms^2)")], 4)
#>      rooms I(rooms^2) 
#>    -0.5451     0.0623

\frac{\Delta\widehat{\log(price)}}{\Delta\, rooms} = -0.545 + 2(0.0623)\,rooms \qquad\Rightarrow\qquad rooms^* = \frac{0.545}{0.1246} = 4.4

  • Going from 5 to 6 rooms: \;-54.5 + 12.4(5) = 7.5\%. From 6 to 7: \;19.9\%.
  • Fewer than 4.4 rooms happens for a handful of communities, so in practice the effect is positive and increasing.

Models with interaction terms

y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_1 + \beta_2 x_2 + \beta_3\,\underbrace{x_1 x_2}_{\text{interaction}} + u \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \frac{\partial y}{\partial x_1} = \beta_1 + \beta_3 x_2

  • The effect of x_1 depends on the level of x_2 (and vice versa).
  • \hat\beta_1 alone is the effect when x_2 = 0, which is often meaningless.

Fix: center the interaction. Replace x_1x_2 by x_1(x_2 - \bar x_2). Then the coefficient on x_1 is the partial effect at the average x_2, and its t statistic tests exactly that effect.

Example: attendance and final exam score

attend (n = 680): stndfnl on attendance rate, prior GPA, ACT, their squares and priGPA \times atndrte.

data("attend")
ai <- lm(stndfnl ~ atndrte + priGPA + ACT + I(priGPA^2) + I(ACT^2) +
           priGPA:atndrte, data = attend)
round(summary(ai)$coefficients[c("atndrte", "atndrte:priGPA"), ], 5)
#>                Estimate Std. Error  t value Pr(>|t|)
#> atndrte        -0.00671    0.01023 -0.65607  0.51201
#> atndrte:priGPA  0.00559    0.00432  1.29382  0.19617
  • \hat\beta_{atndrte} = -0.0067 with t = -0.66: this is the effect at priGPA = 0, which does not exist in the data.
  • The partial effect at the mean priGPA = 2.59 is \;-0.0067 + 0.0056(2.59) = 0.0077.

The centered model makes it testable

attend$pg <- attend$priGPA - mean(attend$priGPA)     # centered
ac <- lm(stndfnl ~ atndrte + priGPA + ACT + I(priGPA^2) + I(ACT^2) +
           atndrte:pg, data = attend)
round(summary(ac)$coefficients["atndrte", ], 5)
#>   Estimate Std. Error    t value   Pr(>|t|) 
#>    0.00774    0.00263    2.93844    0.00341

Now the coefficient on atndrte is the effect at the average prior GPA: 0.0077 with t = 2.94, so it is significant at the 1% level.

Interpretation: for a student with average prior GPA, a 10 percentage point higher attendance rate raises the standardized final score by about 0.077 standard deviations.

Interactive: how the effect varies

Move the slider from 1 to 4: the partial effect of attendance rises with prior GPA, and the confidence band is narrowest near the mean of priGPA, where the data are. At low priGPA the effect is not significantly different from zero. This also explains why the raw coefficient on atndrte (the effect at priGPA = 0) has a t statistic of only -0.66.

Goodness of fit and prediction

Adjusted R^2, predicting E(y\mid x), and the much wider interval for an individual outcome.

Adjusted R^2

R^2 never falls when a regressor is added, so it cannot be used to choose between models with different numbers of regressors.

\bar R^2 = 1 - \frac{SSR/(n-k-1)}{SST/(n-1)} = 1 - (1-R^2)\,\frac{n-1}{n-k-1}

  • It imposes a penalty for extra parameters and can fall.
  • \bar R^2 rises when a variable is added if and only if its |t| > 1; for a group, if and only if its F > 1.
  • Useful for comparing non-nested models, e.g. y = \beta_0 + \beta_1\log(x) vs. y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x + \beta_2 x^2.

Two rules

The dependent variable must be the same (y vs. \log y cannot be compared), and a higher \bar R^2 is never a reason to include a variable that theory rejects.

Prediction

We want to predict y at given values x_1 = c_1, \dots, x_k = c_k:

\hat\theta_0 = \hat\beta_0 + \hat\beta_1 c_1 + \dots + \hat\beta_k c_k \qquad \text{estimates}\qquad \theta_0 = E(y \mid x = c)

How to get its standard error without matrix algebra: write \beta_0 = \theta_0 - \beta_1c_1 - \dots - \beta_kc_k and substitute:

y = \theta_0 + \beta_1 (x_1 - c_1) + \dots + \beta_k (x_k - c_k) + u

Regress y on the centered regressors: the intercept is \hat\theta_0 and R prints its standard error directly.

Example: predicting college GPA (gpa2)

data("gpa2")
g <- lm(colgpa ~ sat + hsperc + hsize + I(hsize^2), data = gpa2)
nd <- data.frame(sat = 1200, hsperc = 30, hsize = 5)
predict(g, nd, interval = "confidence")   # for the average student of this type
#>        fit      lwr      upr
#> 1 2.700075 2.661104 2.739047

\hat y = 2.70 with a 95% CI of [2.66, 2.74] for the average GPA of students with these characteristics.

Where is the interval narrowest?

\text{Var}(\hat y) is smallest at the means of the regressors and grows as c_j moves away from them. Predicting far outside the data is risky.

Predicting an individual outcome

For a new individual, \;y^0 = \beta_0 + \beta_1x_1^0 + \dots + \beta_kx_k^0 + u^0, the prediction error is \hat e^0 = y^0 - \hat y^0 and

\text{Var}(\hat e^0) = \text{Var}(\hat y^0) + \sigma^2 \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \text{se}(\hat e^0) = \sqrt{\text{se}(\hat y^0)^2 + \hat\sigma^2} 

predict(g, nd, interval = "prediction")   # for one individual student
#>        fit      lwr      upr
#> 1 2.700075 1.601749 3.798402

The interval [1.60, 3.80] is much wider: \hat\sigma = 0.56 dominates \text{se}(\hat y^0) = 0.02. More data shrinks the first term but not \hat\sigma.

Interactive: two very different intervals

Check your understanding

A university wants to predict the GPA of one particular incoming student. Which interval should it report?

A) The confidence interval for E(y\mid x), because it is more precise

B) The prediction interval, because a single student also carries the unobserved u^0

C) Either one: they answer the same question

The CI is about the average GPA of all students with these characteristics. For one student we must also account for their individual u^0, which makes the interval much wider.

Key takeaways

  1. Rescaling changes coefficients, not t statistics, R^2 or conclusions.
  2. Beta coefficients make effects comparable across variables.
  3. In quadratic models the effect is \hat\beta_1 + 2\hat\beta_2 x, with turning point |\hat\beta_1/(2\hat\beta_2)|.
  4. With interactions the effect of x_1 depends on x_2; center the interaction to test the effect at the mean.
  5. \bar R^2 penalizes extra regressors and can fall; compare only models with the same y.
  6. CI for E(y|x) ≠ prediction interval for an individual: the latter also contains \hat\sigma^2.
